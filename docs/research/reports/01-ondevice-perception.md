# Track 01: Can post-game analysis run fully offline on a phone?

**Verdict: build an offline-first hybrid.** A phone can decode the gallery video, calibrate the court, track players, estimate pose, analyze audio, and create a match summary without a network. But the evidence does **not** support promising accurate ball or shuttle tracking across all six sports, or analysis under two minutes for a five-minute clip. Ball tracking is the bottleneck: there are good published models, but no verified latency or accuracy results for those models on the target phones and low-angle footage.

A shipped app shows that one-camera tennis tracking on an iPhone is technically possible. SwingVision and an ITF report describe 60 fps phone video processed for ball, court, and player tracking. That does not prove its pipeline is fully offline, generalizes across six sports, or meets our gallery-import timing target. [SwingVision and Apple’s Neural Engine](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2022/05/swupnil-sahai-and-his-co-founder-serve-an-ace-with-ai-powered-swingvision/), [ITF approval report](https://www.itftennis.com/media/15294/pat-25-037-approval-report-swingvision.pdf)

I use **verified** for current API documentation and published measurements. **Estimated** numbers below are arithmetic scenarios, not device benchmarks. Missing target-device evidence is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

## Five-minute timing arithmetic

A five-minute 1080p60 clip has **300 × 60 = 18,000 frames**. For this estimate, run player detection at 5 fps (**1,500 images**) and pose at 10 fps (**3,000 images**). Keep the ball pass at 60 fps.

| Device evidence | Player detection | Pose | Player + pose compute | Add ball at 5 ms/frame | At 10 ms/frame | At 20 ms/frame |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| iPhone 15 Pro class, using iPhone 17 Pro/A19 Pro measurements as a **proxy** | 1,500 × 3.2 ms = **4.8 s** | 3,000 × 3.9 ms = **11.7 s** | **16.5 s** | **106.5 s** | **196.5 s** | **376.5 s** |
| Snapdragon 8 Gen 3, Qualcomm QNN measurements | 1,500 × 2.93 ms = **4.4 s** | 3,000 × 3.841 ms = **11.5 s** | **15.9 s** | **105.9 s** | **195.9 s** | **375.9 s** |

The **5, 10, and 20 ms ball figures are scenario inputs, not measured performance or a claimed expected range**. No cited phone test establishes that these models hit any of those rates. The accessory totals also exclude decode, court fitting, audio analysis, file I/O, and thermal throttling. The iPhone numbers use an A19 Pro measurement, not an iPhone 15 Pro benchmark. The Snapdragon timings are model inference results, not an end-to-end video pipeline benchmark.

For a five-minute clip, the ball stage would need to average about **15.8 ms/frame or faster** to keep model compute below the clip duration, after the measured player and pose passes. For the two-minute target, it needs about **5.8 ms/frame or faster**. The decoder must also deliver 18,000 frames within the target: at least 60 fps for five minutes, or 150 fps for two minutes. Those decode rates are **UNVERIFIED** on the named devices. A pipelined app can overlap decoding and inference; actual wall time still depends on which stage is slower.

On Pixel 10/Tensor G5, the published LiteRT GPU timings give **68.3 seconds** for 1,500 player detections and **139.8 seconds** for 3,000 pose images, about **208 seconds before ball analysis**. This is one Tensor G5 device and model path, not a result for all Tensor phones. Snapdragon 7 Gen 3 and iPhone 15 Pro YOLO26 timings are **UNVERIFIED**.

The three-, ten-, and fifteen-minute timings scale roughly with decoded frame count. At 60 fps, fifteen minutes is **54,000 frames**, three times the five-minute ball workload.

A court homography maps points on the court plane. It does not by itself recover the height and metric 3D path of an airborne ball. Treat speed derived from image motion as a projected estimate unless the camera geometry and depth assumptions are validated. This follows from using a planar homography for a 3D flight path. [OpenCV homography documentation](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d7/dff/tutorial_feature_homography.html)

## Hybrid split and API shape

Keep the phone as the usable product when offline:

- Decode the gallery video locally.
- Let the player choose the sport and confirm court corners.
- Build player paths, available pose signals, audio events, and a timestamped match timeline locally.
- Run Apple Vision trajectories or a supported ball model as an experimental enhancement, with confidence and gaps visible.
- Generate summaries from recorded events, not imagined video details.

When connectivity is available, an optional analysis API can return a ball-track enhancement in the same event schema. Keep the local match record authoritative and queue the request rather than blocking the app. Include source frame, timestamp, sport, court calibration, model/version, confidence, and gaps with each result. The app should still demo if the API is unreachable. No hosting provider was specified, so its current free-tier limits and deployment cost are **UNVERIFIED**.

For research evaluation, RacketVision is useful for checking three sports and includes a median-background preprocessing path. Its 7.54 GB dataset is marked MIT on Hugging Face, but the paper describes YouTube broadcast sources. Verify asset-level reuse rights before using those clips in the public demo. [Dataset card and size](https://huggingface.co/datasets/linfeng302/RacketVision), [AAAI paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/37362/41324)

## Recommendation

Build the demo around offline video import, court-corner confirmation, player paths, event review, and a polished evidence timeline. Add ball tracking only where the clip and model pass confidence checks. Keep the optional API for enhanced processing when online; do not make the demo depend on it or claim a reliable six-sport, fully offline system.

## Three biggest risks

1. **Ball tracking does not transfer to the demo view.** Published results are mainly broadcast or research footage, not low-angle phone video from a fence or tripod.
2. **Ball latency and sustained decode are unknown.** Hitting the two-minute target requires roughly 5.8 ms per ball frame plus fast enough video decode; no target-device benchmark proves it.
3. **Sport coverage and model rights are incomplete.** The cited ball weights cover three sports. YOLO is AGPL-3.0, and separate licenses for several ball checkpoints are **UNVERIFIED**.
